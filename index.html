1️⃣ ORGANIC CHEMISTRY

Definition

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals mainly with carbon-containing compounds, including their structures, properties, preparation, reactions and uses.

Why carbon is important

Carbon has four valence electrons and normally forms four covalent bonds. It can bond to other carbon atoms and to many other elements.

Catenation

Catenation is the ability of carbon atoms to form covalent bonds with one another to produce long chains, branched chains and rings.

Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.

The major classes include:

  • Alkanes
  • Alkenes
  • Alkynes
  • Aromatic hydrocarbons

Homologous series

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds having the same general formula, similar chemical properties and a gradual change in physical properties.

Successive members differ by a CH₂ unit.

Example: CH₄ → C₂H₆ → C₃H₈ → C₄H₁₀

4️⃣ ALKANES — PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND USES

Definition

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen single bonds.

General formula of acyclic alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Examples

  • Methane — CH₄
  • Ethane — C₂H₆
  • Propane — C₃H₈
  • Butane — C₄H₁₀
  • Pentane — C₅H₁₂

Preparation of alkanes

1. Hydrogenation of alkenes

Alkenes can be converted to alkanes by addition of hydrogen in the presence of a catalyst such as nickel, platinum or palladium.

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃–CH₃
Ethene → Ethane

2. Decarboxylation

A sodium salt of a carboxylic acid can be heated with soda lime (NaOH/CaO) to produce an alkane containing one fewer carbon atom.

CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃

3. Wurtz reaction

Alkyl halides can react with sodium in dry ether to form higher alkanes.

2R–X + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX

Physical properties

  • Lower alkanes are gases at room temperature.
  • Intermediate members are liquids.
  • Higher members are solids.
  • They are generally non-polar.
  • They are insoluble in water.
  • They are soluble in many non-polar organic solvents.
  • Boiling point generally increases as molecular size increases.
  • Branching generally lowers boiling point compared with a corresponding straight-chain isomer.

Chemical properties

1. Combustion

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

2. Halogenation

Alkanes undergo free-radical substitution with halogens under suitable conditions, commonly UV light.

Free-radical substitution mechanism

Initiation

Cl₂ → 2Cl•

Propagation
Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃•
CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•

Termination
Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl
CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆

Uses of alkanes

  • Methane is used as a fuel and is a major component of natural gas.
  • Propane and butane are used as LPG fuels.
  • Higher alkanes are components of petrol, kerosene and diesel fractions.
  • Some higher alkanes are used in lubricants.
  • Paraffin wax is used in candles and other applications.
5️⃣ ALKENES — PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND USES

Definition

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

General formula of simple acyclic alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ

Preparation

1. Dehydration of alcohols

An alcohol can lose water when heated with a suitable dehydrating acid such as concentrated sulfuric acid, or under suitable catalytic conditions.

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
Ethanol → Ethene

2. Dehydrohalogenation of haloalkanes

A hydrogen halide can be eliminated from a haloalkane using a suitable base, commonly alcoholic KOH in classical laboratory preparation.

CH₃CH₂Br → CH₂=CH₂ + HBr

3. Cracking of larger hydrocarbons

Large hydrocarbons can be broken into smaller hydrocarbons, producing alkenes among the products.

Physical properties

  • Lower alkenes are generally gases.
  • Higher members become liquids and then solids.
  • They are generally non-polar.
  • They are poorly soluble in water.
  • They are soluble in many organic solvents.
  • Boiling point generally increases with molecular mass.

Chemical properties

1. Hydrogenation

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃

2. Halogenation

CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂Br–CH₂Br

3. Addition of hydrogen halides

CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br

4. Hydration

Water can be added across a carbon-carbon double bond under suitable acid-catalyzed conditions to form an alcohol.

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH

5. Oxidation

Alkenes can undergo various oxidation reactions depending on the reagent and conditions.

Electrophilic addition mechanism

The π bond is electron-rich.

Step 1: The alkene attacks an electrophile.

Step 2: A carbocation or related intermediate may form, depending on the reaction.

Step 3: A nucleophile attacks the intermediate.

Markovnikov's rule

In many additions of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene, hydrogen adds to the carbon already bearing more hydrogen atoms, while X becomes attached to the more substituted carbon.

Uses

  • Ethene is used to manufacture polyethylene.
  • Ethene is an important starting material for ethanol production.
  • Alkenes are used to produce polymers.
  • They are important raw materials in the petrochemical industry.
6️⃣ ALKYNES — PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND USES

Definition

Alkynes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.

General formula of simple acyclic alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

Example

HC≡CH
Ethyne

Preparation

1. From calcium carbide

Ethyne can be prepared by reacting calcium carbide with water.

CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂ + Ca(OH)₂

2. Double dehydrohalogenation

Alkynes can be produced by successive elimination reactions from suitable dihaloalkanes under appropriate conditions.

Physical properties

  • Lower alkynes are gases.
  • Higher members have higher boiling points.
  • They are generally non-polar.
  • They are poorly soluble in water.
  • They dissolve in many organic solvents.

Chemical properties

1. Hydrogenation

HC≡CH + H₂ → CH₂=CH₂
CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃

2. Addition of halogens

Halogens can add across the triple bond.

3. Addition of hydrogen halides

HX can add to an alkyne under suitable conditions.

4. Combustion

Alkynes burn in oxygen and can release substantial heat.

Uses

  • Ethyne has been used in oxyacetylene welding and cutting.
  • Alkynes are useful intermediates in organic synthesis.
  • They are used in the manufacture of various organic chemicals.
7️⃣ ALCOHOLS — PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND USES

Definition

Alcohols are organic compounds containing a hydroxyl group (–OH) attached to a saturated carbon atom.

Ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH

Classification

Primary alcohol

The carbon bearing the OH group is attached to one other carbon atom.

Secondary alcohol

The carbon bearing the OH group is attached to two other carbon atoms.

Tertiary alcohol

The carbon bearing the OH group is attached to three other carbon atoms.

Preparation

1. Hydration of alkenes

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH

2. Fermentation

Ethanol can be produced by fermentation of sugars using yeast under suitable conditions.

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

3. Hydrolysis of haloalkanes

R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻

Physical properties

  • Small alcohols are generally colorless liquids.
  • Lower alcohols are appreciably soluble in water.
  • Solubility generally decreases as the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer.
  • Alcohols have relatively high boiling points because of hydrogen bonding.

Chemical properties

1. Reaction with sodium

2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂

2. Oxidation

Primary alcohols can generally be oxidized first to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids under suitable conditions.

Primary alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Secondary alcohols can generally be oxidized to ketones. Tertiary alcohols resist oxidation under many ordinary conditions.

3. Dehydration

Alcohols can lose water to form alkenes under suitable conditions.

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O

Uses

  • Ethanol is used as a solvent.
  • Ethanol is used in some fuels and fuel blends.
  • Alcohols are important industrial solvents and chemical feedstocks.
  • Methanol is used as an industrial chemical and fuel.
  • Some alcohols are used in cosmetics and cleaning products.
8️⃣ HALOALKANES — PREPARATION, PROPERTIES AND USES

Definition

Haloalkanes are compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane have been replaced by halogen atoms.

Halogens include: F, Cl, Br and I.

CH₃Cl — chloromethane
CH₃CH₂Br — bromoethane

Preparation

1. Free-radical halogenation of alkanes

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
UV light

2. Reaction of alcohols with hydrogen halides

R–OH + HBr → R–Br + H₂O

3. Reaction with phosphorus halides

Suitable phosphorus halides can convert alcohols into haloalkanes.

Physical properties

  • Many are colorless liquids or gases depending on molecular size.
  • They are generally poorly soluble in water.
  • They are soluble in many organic solvents.
  • Boiling points generally increase with increasing molecular mass.

Chemical properties

1. Nucleophilic substitution

R–Br + OH⁻ → R–OH + Br⁻

2. Elimination

A haloalkane can lose HX to produce an alkene under suitable conditions.

CH₃CH₂Br → CH₂=CH₂ + HBr

Uses

  • Some haloalkanes are used as organic synthesis intermediates.
  • Some have been used as solvents.
  • Some halogenated compounds have specialized industrial uses.
  • They are important starting materials in organic synthesis.
Safety: The uses of individual halogenated compounds depend strongly on their toxicity and environmental properties. Some historical halogenated substances have been restricted or phased out.
9️⃣ AROMATIC CHEMISTRY

Benzene

Benzene has the molecular formula C₆H₆ and contains a six-membered aromatic ring with delocalized π electrons.

Aromaticity

Aromatic compounds possess a special type of stabilization associated with cyclic conjugated π-electron systems.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution

Benzene commonly undergoes substitution reactions that preserve the aromatic system.

Nitration

C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Sulfuric acid helps generate the nitronium ion, NO₂⁺, the electrophile in nitration.

Step 1: Generation of electrophile.

Step 2: Benzene attacks the electrophile.

Step 3: A positively charged intermediate forms.

Step 4: Loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

Other reactions

  • Halogenation
  • Sulfonation
  • Friedel-Crafts alkylation
  • Friedel-Crafts acylation
🔟 ORGANIC REACTION MECHANISMS

Reaction mechanism

A reaction mechanism is a step-by-step description of how bonds break and form during a chemical reaction.

Electrophiles

Electrophiles are electron-pair acceptors.

Examples include H⁺ and NO₂⁺.

Nucleophiles

Nucleophiles are electron-pair donors.

Examples include OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃ and H₂O.

Homolytic fission

A–B → A• + B•

Each atom receives one electron from the broken bond.

Heterolytic fission

A–B → A⁺ + B⁻

Both bonding electrons go to one atom.

Free radicals

Free radicals contain an unpaired electron.

Carbocations

Carbocations are positively charged carbon species.

Carbanions

Carbanions are negatively charged carbon species.

Curved arrows

A full curved arrow shows movement of an electron pair. A fishhook arrow shows movement of one electron.

Major reaction types

  • Addition
  • Substitution
  • Elimination
  • Oxidation
  • Reduction
  • Condensation
  • Hydrolysis
1️⃣1️⃣ ISOMERISM

Definition

Isomers are compounds having the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.

Structural isomerism

Structural isomers have different connectivity of atoms.

C₄H₁₀:
Butane
2-methylpropane

Types of structural isomerism

  • Chain isomerism
  • Position isomerism
  • Functional-group isomerism

Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different three-dimensional arrangements.

They include configurational and conformational isomerism.

1️⃣2️⃣ STEREOCHEMISTRY

Definition

Stereochemistry is the study of the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in molecules.

Geometrical isomerism

Geometrical isomerism results from restricted rotation, commonly around a carbon-carbon double bond or within a ring.

Cis

Relevant similar groups are on the same side.

Trans

Relevant similar groups are on opposite sides.

E/Z system

The E/Z system is used when each carbon of a double bond has two different substituents.

Optical isomerism

Optical isomerism involves stereoisomers related as non-superimposable mirror images.

Enantiomers

Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror-image stereoisomers.

Conformational isomerism

Conformers are different spatial arrangements produced by rotation around single bonds without breaking the sigma bond.

1️⃣3️⃣ CHIRALITY, STEREOGENIC CENTERS AND R/S

Chirality

A molecule is chiral when it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image.

Chiral center

A common chiral center is a tetrahedral carbon attached to four different groups.

Stereogenic center

A stereogenic center is an atom at which exchange of two attached groups produces a stereoisomer.

Assigning R/S

  1. Assign priorities 1–4.
  2. Higher atomic number generally receives higher priority.
  3. Place priority 4 pointing away.
  4. Trace 1 → 2 → 3.
  5. Clockwise = R.
  6. Counterclockwise = S.
If priority 4 points toward you, reverse the apparent result.

Example of priority

Br > Cl > F > H

This order follows atomic number.

1️⃣4️⃣ LABORATORY CHEMISTRY AND MELTING POINT

Melting point

The melting point is the temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid under specified conditions.

Importance

  • Identification of compounds
  • Purity testing
  • Comparison with known substances

Pure substance

A pure crystalline compound usually melts over a relatively narrow temperature range.

Impure substance

An impurity generally lowers the melting point and broadens the melting range.

Capillary method

  1. Powder the sample.
  2. Place a small amount into a capillary tube.
  3. Pack the sample properly.
  4. Attach the capillary appropriately to the thermometer.
  5. Heat gradually.
  6. Record the temperature at which melting begins.
  7. Record the temperature at which melting is complete.
If melting begins at 78°C and is complete at 82°C:
Melting range = 78–82°C
